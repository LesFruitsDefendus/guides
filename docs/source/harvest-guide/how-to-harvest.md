# How to harvest and store fruit

## Harvesting fruit

Most fruit grows each year on the same parts of the branch (at the "spur"; see **Fig 1** ). If you damage the spur while picking the fruit, you may compromise future harvests. Moreover, how fruit is picked can influence its conservation once stored.
Keep in mind that you have less chance of damaging the tree when you pick the fruit correctly and at the right time.
Follow these **general harvesting tips** to ensure the protection of the tree and its fruit:

- Fruit should be picked with the stem, but without the spur.
- Never cut the stem of the fruit.
- Never pull fruit downward. It is always better to lift or twist it to detach it from the branch. When fruit is ready, a natural break occurs at the abscission zone—the specialized area between the fruit and the branch that allows the tree to shed fruit.
- Do not do anything which damages the bark of the tree. It is its circulatory system!
- For more information about fruiting structures, see [this reference.](https://www.pomologie.fr/elements-vegetaux-des-fruitiers-identifier-pour-mieux-tailler-3/)
- Avoid cutting, damaging, or removing branches, especially in summer. Even if the owner asks you to do so, be aware that this is harmful to the tree at this time of year because it can invite disease. Pruning must be done at the right point in the tree’s growth cycle.
- When using a picking bucket, hang it around your neck. Do not fill it completely, or some fruit may be crushed and other fruit may fall out.
- When using any kind of tools, especially those that come in direct contact with branches, ALWAYS sterilise your tools between harvests (at the end of the harvest, or before moving on to the next property) in order to ensure that there is no risk of spreading bacterial or fungal infections between the trees in our network.

### Apples and crabapples

#### Harvest period
In Montréal, the apple and crabapple season generally runs from late August to October.

- Early apples: mid-July to mid-August
- Mid-season apples: September to October
- Crabapples: mid-August to mid-September

Maturity varies considerably depending on the cultivar and the urban micro-site.

#### How to recognize the right stage of maturity

**Apples**
- Colour has developed according to the cultivar.
- The fruit is firm but detaches easily when lifted.
- The flavour has a good balance of sweetness and acidity.
- The seeds are dark brown, which is an indicator of internal maturity.

**Crabapples**
- Bright colour (red, yellow, or orange, depending on the variety).
- Fruit is slightly soft.
- Fruit remains attached to the tree, but maturity is often uneven.

**Leave on the tree:**
- Very green, hard fruit.
- Immature fruit with a very astringent taste.

**Remember:** Not all fruit on the same tree ripens at the same time. You may need to visit a tree several times over one to two weeks as the fruit matures, so you can harvest at the right time.

#### Harvesting techniques

Apples should be picked with a twisting motion accompanied by a gentle lift; ideally, by pressing on the stem with the tip of your finger to ensure that the spur does not tear as the fruit detaches. If the fruit does not come off easily, it is not ripe.
By hand: Turn the apple while slightly raising and pull gently. Do not pull down on the fruit. 
With fruit picking tool: position the metal part around the apple, lift slightly and rotate (see Fig 2). Do not pull down on the fruit.
- Pick by hand, one fruit at a time.
- Lift and twist slightly to detach the fruit; avoid pulling.
- Ideally, do not shake the branches, as this can cause immature fruit to fall and injure the tree.
- Prioritize accessible, ripe fruit, making several passes if needed.

**For tall trees:**
- Use a safe step ladder or suitable picking poles.
- Always secure the area beneath the tree.

#### Sorting

Keep:
- Firm, intact fruit.
- Fruit without punctures or cracks.
- Fruit without soft spots.

Discard:
- Fruit that has fallen to the ground and is damaged.
- Soft, rotten, damaged, or fermented fruit.
- Fruit with insect punctures.
- Fruit with extensive spotting and internal deterioration.

#### Transport and storage
Apples and crabapples are relatively sturdy but sensitive to internal bruising. Do not handle apples like potatoes: they bruise easily, so place them down gently and empty containers carefully.

Recommended:
- Shallow, rigid containers
- A single layer, if possible
- Gentle handling
- Prompt refrigeration (ideally between 4 and 10 degrees)

Storage life
- Several weeks or even months in a cool place, depending on cultivar and maturity.
- Crabapples sometimes keep well for only a few weeks.

#### Distribution notes
- Good potential for storage and gradual redistribution.
- Apples are ideal for distribution over several weeks.
- Crabapples are often best used in processed products (jellies, syrups).
- They are sensitive to impacts, so sorting and careful handling are important.

### Cherries

#### Harvest period
In Montréal, cherry trees generally produce fruit from late June to mid-July.

The harvest window is short and depends heavily on sun exposure. Fruit can go from unripe to overripe within a few days. Cherries do not ripen after picking, so they must be harvested when fully ripe.

#### How to recognize the right stage of maturity

**Sweet cherries**
- Dark red to almost black, depending on the variety
- Shiny, firm but slightly yielding
- Clearly sweet, without strong acidity

**Sour cherries**
- Bright red to dark red
- More acidic flavour, even when ripe
- Fully coloured throughout the fruit

**Leave on the tree:**
- Green fruit
- Partially coloured fruit (pale pink or yellow-red)
- Fruit that is still firm and acidic

Cherries must be harvested as soon as they reach optimum ripeness: waiting until they are overripe significantly increases the risk of the fruit becoming mushy and of fruit flies or their larvae being present. As soon as the fruit begins to ripen, you must take action to harvest at the optimum time.

#### Harvesting techniques

Always pick cherries with the stem intact; otherwise, they will spoil quickly!

By hand: Hold the fruit at the end of the stem. Twist it, or gently pull it sideways or upward until the joint at the end of the stem separates from the branch. Do not pull the stem downward. Do not use scissors to cut the stem.
- Pick by hand, one fruit at a time.
- Keep the stem attached to avoid damaging the flesh and to extend potential storage life.
- Avoid pulling on branches, which are fragile.
- Make several passes during the season.

For high branches:
- Use a secure step ladder or suitable picking poles, if available.
- Prioritize safety during harvesting.

#### Sorting
Keep:
- Well-coloured, firm fruit
- Fruit without cracks or injuries
- Fruit that is dry to the touch

Discard
- Green or partially ripe fruit
- Split fruit (after rain)
- Soft, fermented, or mouldy fruit
- Fruit badly damaged by birds

#### Transport and storage

Cherries are fragile and easily crushed.

Recommended:
- Small, shallow, rigid containers
- Very shallow layers of fruit
- Prompt refrigeration after picking

**Avoid:**
- Soft bags
- Piling fruit
- Prolonged exposure to heat

Storage life
- 2–5 days in the refrigerator
- Freezing is possible; pitting them first is ideal

### Mulberries

#### Harvest period

In Montréal, mulberries generally ripen from early July to mid-August, depending on the species, sun exposure, and weather.

Unlike many other tree fruits, the harvest can extend over several weeks because fruit on the same tree does not ripen all at once. Several visits per week may be needed during this period to pick fruit at its best.

#### How to recognize the right stage of maturity

Mulberries are ready to pick when they reach their mature colour and detach easily from their stems. They start to fall as soon as they are ripe.

Ready to pick
- Black, dark purple, or dark red fruit, depending on the variety.
- Soft and juicy fruit.
- Fruit that detaches easily to the touch.

**Leave on the tree**
- Green fruit.
- White or pink fruit (for dark-fruited varieties).
- Firm fruit that is difficult to detach.

Mulberries do not continue to ripen significantly after picking, so it is important to harvest them when fully ripe.

#### Species-specific characteristics

Mulberry trees can be especially productive and may yield very large quantities of fruit.

Ripe fruit falls easily to the ground, which can make for abundant but short-lived harvests. Beneath some trees, the ground can quickly become covered with fallen fruit.

Mulberries naturally fall as soon as they reach full maturity. A significant proportion of the crop can therefore end up on the ground within just a few days, especially after wind or rain.

Birds are very fond of mulberries and may consume a large part of the crop.

The fruit stains hands, clothing, and surfaces heavily. Wear clothes that you do not mind staining during harvesting activities.

#### Harvesting techniques

Mulberries can be picked by hand when they are within easy reach, but this method is generally inefficient for tall trees.

For large trees, a commonly used method is to:
- Spread or hold a clean tarp beneath one main branch at a time.
- Gently shake the branch so ripe fruit falls onto the tarp.
- Move the tarp to another main branch and repeat.
- Collect the fruit that has fallen onto the tarp.

This technique makes it possible to harvest large quantities efficiently while limiting handling.

Avoid shaking branches too vigorously, so that unripe fruit does not fall.

#### Sorting

Keep:
- Fully ripe fruit.
- Fruit that is still firm despite being ripe.
- Clean, intact fruit.

Discard:
- Green or insufficiently ripe fruit.
- Dried-out fruit.
- Fruit showing mould.
- Crushed or fermenting fruit.

When harvesting with a tarp, remove leaves, twigs, and other plant debris before storage.

#### Transport and storage

Mulberries are fragile and do not keep for long after picking.

Recommended:
- Use shallow containers.
- Prefer small, rigid, reused containers such as those used for strawberries, raspberries, or blueberries.
- Avoid crushing the fruit under its own weight.
- Refrigerate promptly after harvesting.

**Avoid**
- Soft bags
- Containers that are too deep
- Excessive stacking of fruit

Storage life
- A few days in the refrigerator.
- Freezing is possible.
- Prompt processing is recommended for large harvests.

Mulberries are popular, but their storage life is limited.

It is recommended that you:
- Distribute them quickly after harvesting.
- Avoid excessive handling.
- Tell recipients that the fruit should be eaten or processed quickly. Mulberries are particularly well suited to community kitchens, community fridges, and food-processing initiatives.

Large harvests are especially suitable for group processing (jams, jellies, and freezing).

### Plums

#### Harvest period

In Montréal, the plum season is long, generally running from mid-July (e.g., ‘Early Golden’) to late October (e.g., ‘Mont-Royal’). However, most plum trees are ready for harvest in August.

Ripening progresses differently depending on the cultivar and site. Some trees may therefore be ready for harvest over several weeks, with different peak periods for different varieties.

#### How to recognize the right stage of maturity

Plums should be picked when fully ripe; they do not ripen significantly after picking.

Ready to pick
- The colour typical of the cultivar (yellow, red, purple, or dark blue)
- Fruit that is slightly soft to the touch
- Fruit that detaches easily from the branch (for some cultivars)
- A developed sweet flavour, or a balanced sweet-acid flavour depending on the variety

**Leave on the tree**
- Green or very firm fruit
- Fruit that is still acidic and not very colourful

Overripe fruit softens quickly, and the risk of spotted-wing drosophila (fruit flies) increases. This can lead to internal deterioration and the presence of larvae.

#### Harvesting techniques

Plums can be picked in the same way as apples. A ripe plum detaches easily.

By hand: Gently turn the plum upward. Do not pull the fruit downward.

With a fruit picker: Position the metal part around the plum, lift gently, and twist. Do not pull the fruit downward.
- Pick by hand, one fruit at a time.
- Twist the fruit slightly to detach it without damaging the stem or branch.
- Avoid pulling on fragile twigs.
- Prioritize ripe fruit only; several passes may be needed.

For larger trees:
- Use a safe step ladder or suitable tools, such as telescopic picking poles, if available.
- Very gently shake branches to drop ripe fruit onto a tarp suspended beneath the branch.

#### Sorting

Keep:
- Fully ripe fruit
- Fruit that is firm but slightly yielding
- Intact fruit without cracks

Discard:
- Green or unripe fruit
- Split fruit (after rain)
- Soft or fermented fruit
- Fruit with visible mould or larvae

#### Transport and storage

Plums are moderately fragile but become sensitive to crushing once ripe.

Recommended:
- Shallow, rigid containers
- Avoid piling; store in limited layers
- Prompt refrigeration

**Avoid**
- Soft bags
- High stacks of fruit

Storage life
- A few days in the refrigerator.
- Freezing is possible.
- Prompt processing is recommended for surplus fruit.

#### Distribution notes
- Plums are popular but have a relatively short shelf life.
- Distribute them quickly while they are fresh.
- They are well suited to group processing during peak production.
- They are sensitive to excessive handling.
- Plums do not ripen after picking.

### Pears

#### Harvest period

In Montréal, pears are generally harvested from late August to October, depending on the cultivar. Many varieties grow in urban areas. Maturity varies considerably between trees and extends over several weeks.

#### How to recognize the right stage of maturity

Pears should ideally be harvested **before they are fully ripe**, as they ripen after harvest.

Ready to pick
- The fruit is firm but detaches easily when lifted slightly.
- The background colour is beginning to turn yellow or change (depending on the cultivar).
- The flesh is still firm, not soft on the tree.
- The fruit has reached its full size.

**Leave on the tree**
- Fruit that is very hard and green and has not yet reached its full size.

Key field test: Lift the fruit slightly. If it detaches without force, it is ready.

#### Species-specific characteristics
- There is considerable variation between cultivars in texture, sweetness, and maturity.
- Many urban pear trees are older or unidentified varieties.
- Fruit does not ripen properly on the tree, so harvesting at the right time is important.
- Some varieties are very prone to dropping prematurely.
- Production is often abundant but concentrated in a short window for each tree.
- Pear trees often show biennial bearing.

#### Harvesting techniques

Pears can be picked in the same way as apples (see the section above), but remember that they should be harvested before they are fully ripe. This can sometimes make picking a little more difficult. Take extra care not to pull the fruit, so you do not damage the spur.

- Pick by hand, one fruit at a time.
- Lift and twist slightly to detach the fruit; do not pull.
- Avoid impacts, as pears are very susceptible to bruising.
- Prioritize accessible fruit and make several passes.

For taller trees:
- Use a safe step ladder or telescopic picking poles.
- Shaking branches is not recommended because it can cause fruit to fall, injure people, and damage the tree. Only do this if it is the only practical way to harvest the fruit and you have a safe way to catch the falling fruit in a tarp. In that case, the fruit is more likely to be damaged and may be better suited to processing.

#### Sorting

Keep:
- Firm, intact fruit
- Fruit without bruises or punctures
- Clean fruit with intact skin

Discard:
- Fruit that is soft on the tree (too far advanced)
- Fallen and damaged fruit
- Fruit with bruises, punctures, or mould
- Very green, unripe fruit

#### Transport and storage
Pears are **very sensitive to impacts**, even when they seem firm.

Recommended:
- Shallow, rigid containers. Bags may be acceptable if the fruit is handled gently
- Ideally, a single layer
- Minimal handling
- Prompt refrigeration

**Important characteristic:**

Pears ripen after harvest (over a few days at room temperature or under controlled conditions). Their texture deteriorates quickly if they ripen on the tree.

#### Distribution notes
- Pears are often harvested while firm, so recipients may need an explanation.
- They can keep very well if harvested correctly.
- They are ideal for gradual distribution because ripening can be staggered.
- Take care during transport: damage from impacts may not be visible at first but can lead to rapid deterioration.

### Saskatoon berries (serviceberries)

#### Harvest period
Saskatoon berries are generally the first tree fruits to ripen during the summer in Montréal. Harvest usually takes place between late June and early July.

The optimal harvest window is often short and may vary with the weather. During hot, dry periods, fruit can ripen very quickly and may even begin to dry out on the tree if harvesting is delayed.

#### How to recognize the right stage of maturity
Ready to pick
- Purple or dark violet fruit
- Fruit that is slightly soft
- Sweet, fragrant flavour

**Leave on the tree**
- Green fruit
- Red or pink fruit
- Firm fruit that is not very sweet

Since not all fruit on the same tree necessarily ripens at the same time, it can be helpful to make several passes during the harvest period.

#### Species-specific characteristics
Several species and varieties of serviceberry grow in Montréal. They generally grow as small trees, shrubs, or suckering trees (sometimes trained to have a single trunk). Fruit size, flavour, texture, and productivity can vary from one tree to another.

Birds are particularly fond of serviceberries and can consume a large part of the crop within just a few days. Regularly monitoring trees as the fruit approaches maturity is therefore recommended.

These small fruits are delicate and have a short shelf life after picking.

#### Harvesting techniques
Pick serviceberries gently by hand to avoid crushing them.
- Harvest only ripe fruit.
- Handle fruit carefully to preserve its quality.
- Avoid pulling excessively on branches.
- When possible, make several successive harvests rather than one early harvest.

Serviceberries are an important food source for many urban bird and small-mammal species. When quantities allow, it is recommended to leave some of the harvest for wildlife.

#### Sorting
There is generally little need for sorting when fruit is picked directly from the tree.

Keep:
- Ripe, clean, well-formed fruit

Discard:
- Dried-out fruit
- Damaged or crushed fruit
- Fruit showing signs of mould
- Fruit with significant abnormalities

It is preferable to leave inedible fruit on the tree rather than pick it.

#### Transport and storage
Serviceberries are easily crushed and must be transported carefully.

Recommended containers
- Small, shallow, rigid containers
- Reused containers for strawberries, raspberries, or blueberries

**Avoid**
- Soft bags
- Containers that are too deep
- Excessive stacking of fruit

After harvesting:
- Keep fruit in the shade as much as possible.
- Refrigerate promptly.
- Eat or distribute within the following few days.
- Fruit can also be frozen for later use.

#### Distribution notes
Because they are fragile and have a short shelf life, serviceberries should ideally be redistributed soon after harvesting.

Pay particular attention to packaging and transport to avoid crushing the fruit.

When possible, aim to distribute them within 24–48 hours of picking.

### Grapes

#### Harvest period
In Montréal, grapes generally ripen between late August and October, depending on the cultivar and exposure.

The most common varieties in urban areas are hardy hybrids (often derived from Vitis labrusca), which are more cold-resistant than classic Vitis vinifera grape varieties. Riverbank grape (Vitis riparia) is a wild, native species in Québec, but its small fruits are generally more appealing to wildlife than to people.

Ripening progresses over time and can vary on the same vine.

#### How to recognize the right stage of maturity
Grapes should be harvested when fully ripe, as they do not continue to ripen after picking.

Ready to pick:
- Uniform colour according to the cultivar (golden green, red, or dark purple)
- Berries that are yielding but still firm
- A well-developed sweet flavour, with less acidity
- Grapes that detach easily from the cluster stem

Leave on the vine:
- Green or acidic berries
- Very firm, small fruit
- Clusters with uneven colour (incomplete ripening)

#### Species-specific characteristics
- Ripening is often uneven within a single cluster.
- There is considerable variation between cultivars in acidity, size, and skin thickness.
- Some urban vines produce very large crops, but production varies from year to year.
- Birds may consume a large part of the crop once the grapes ripen.
- Clusters can dry out or deteriorate quickly at the end of the season.

#### Harvesting techniques
By hand: Cut clusters using clean pruning shears or scissors.
- Cut the entire cluster with clean scissors or pruning shears.
- Handle gently to avoid crushing the berries.
- Do not pull on clusters, as this can damage the vine.
- Prioritize fully ripe clusters and avoid mixing fruit at very different stages of maturity.

For tall vines:
- Use a secure step ladder.
- Do not pull on the branches, which are fragile and flexible.

#### Sorting
Keep:
- Healthy, intact clusters
- Firm berries without cracks
- Well-coloured, sweet grapes

Discard:
- Soft or split berries
- Clusters with visible mould (advanced Botrytis)
- Fermented or badly damaged fruit
- Partially dried-out or deteriorated clusters

#### Transport and storage
Grapes are susceptible to crushing and rapid fermentation.

Recommended:
- Small, rigid containers
- Avoid thick layers
- Minimal handling
- Prompt refrigeration

Storage life
- A few days in the refrigerator.
- Prompt consumption is recommended after harvesting.
- Freezing is possible for culinary use.

### Storage

At Santropol Roulant, all fruits except apples can be put in the walk-in fridge in the basement of the building (a.k.a. cold room, cold storage). Apples can be put on the Fruits Défendus shelf unless they are in danger of rotting. Please don't place containers on the floor of the walk-in fridge.

"Soft" fruits (cherries, Saskatoon berries, plums, grapes) should be stored in a way where they do not get crushed under their own weight. Therefore, use small bags and try not to pile the bags on top of each other. If you can, we recommend using recyclable plastic produce containers (rigid packaging used for selling mixed greens, cherry tomatoes, strawberries, etc.) to transport and store soft fruit. Wooden boxes used to sell clementines can also be used for this.

Pears must be stored in a space where the temperature is close to 0 ° C; otherwise they will continue to ripen. Pears stored at room temperature will mature quickly and must be eaten a few days after picking. If unripe pears are stored in the cold, they can be stacked without a problem. However, if they are kept at room temperature, they should not be packed too tightly.

#### In summary:
- Always sterilize harvesting tools (picking poles, bins, etc.) between harvests to avoid spreading tree diseases from one tree to another.
- Make sure you always choose a beneficiary organisation to which you can donate the fruit, depending on its quality and quantity. It is best if their activities are well suited to the type of fruit you are donating (for example, processing organisations for soft fruit or small quantities, or emergency aid organisations for beautiful, perfect fruit).
- Please follow the picking guidelines set out in this guide.
- Never attempt to pick fruit from high branches, dangerous branches or branches near power lines; the safety of the pickers must always be the top priority. Even if the owner asks you to pick all the fruit from their tree, you must explain to them that we only pick fruit that can be reached safely.
- Please give your fruit as soon as possible directly to the recipient.
